The United Nations Global Goals

About SDG 14

Life Below Water is one of the UN’s 17 Sustainable Development Goals. Its 10 targets address how we protect marine ecosystems and use ocean resources, including how we fish, manage waste, and cooperate across borders.

14.1 · Marine pollution
Reduce pollution from land and at sea, including litter and excess nutrients.
14.2 · Healthy ecosystems
Protect, manage, and restore marine and coastal ecosystems.
14.3 · Ocean acidification
Reduce and address acidification, including by working together on research.
14.4 · Sustainable fishing
End overfishing and illegal and destructive fishing. Use science to rebuild fish stocks.
14.5 · Protected areas
Conserve at least 10% of coastal and marine areas, guided by science and law.
14.6 · Fisheries subsidies
Remove harmful subsidies that drive overfishing or support illegal fishing.
14.7 · Sustainable ocean economies
Increase the benefits of sustainable fisheries, aquaculture, and tourism for small island developing states and least developed countries.
14.a · Ocean science
Build research capacity and share marine technology.
14.b · Small-scale fishers
Improve access to marine resources and markets for small-scale fishers.
14.c · International law
Use international law to support conservation and sustainable use of the ocean.

Sunlight Zone

Sunlight fuels life in the upper ocean.

Target 14.2 · Protect ecosystems

A reef full of life

Hawksbill turtles forage for sponges near reefs, while blue-green chromis gather above branching coral. Reefs shelter marine life, support local fisheries, and soften waves before they reach shore.

Protecting reefs from local pressures such as polluted runoff and anchor damage gives them a better chance to recover after heat stress.

Why do corals turn white?

Heat stress can make corals expel the tiny algae that give them color and much of their food. This is coral bleaching. Corals can recover if the water cools soon enough.

Record ocean heat

Ocean heat content reached a record high for the ninth year in a row. As the ocean stores more heat, marine heatwaves can bleach reefs and push some species into cooler waters.

Target 14.3 · Ocean acidification

When seawater absorbs carbon dioxide, its pH falls. That can make it harder for corals and shellfish to build skeletons and shells. Monitoring pH helps countries track the problem and plan responses.

Target 14.1 · Reduce pollution

From street to sea

Rain can wash litter from streets into storm drains, streams, and rivers that empty into the ocean. A bottle dropped far inland can end up where sea turtles feed.

Better waste collection keeps litter out of rivers. Treating wastewater and reducing fertilizer runoff also cut the flow of excess nutrients into coastal waters.

Target 14.5 · Conserve marine areas

Ocean area protected or conserved

About one tenth of the ocean had an official designation. Clear rules, monitoring, and enforcement help those areas protect the habitats and wildlife they were created for.

02 200–1,000 m

Twilight Zone

Only faint traces of sunlight reach this layer.

The nightly migration

Many lanternfish rise at night to feed on plankton near the surface, then retreat into darker water at dawn to avoid daytime predators.

How does this connect to fishing?

Lanternfish carry energy from plankton to larger predators. Science-based catch limits help harvested fish populations replenish and keep prey available across the food web.

Ocean Status

35.5% 2021 assessment

Target 14.4 · End overfishing

Assessed marine fish stocks overfished

The UN’s 2026 report cites a 2021 assessment that found more than one in three assessed stocks were overfished. Depleted populations can mean smaller catches and less reliable income for coastal communities.

03 1,000–4,000 m

Midnight Zone

Sunlight gives way to flashes made by living things.

A light in the dark

Bioluminescence is light produced by living organisms through a chemical reaction. A female black seadevil uses a lure lit by bacteria to draw prey within striking distance.

Target 14.a · Advance ocean science

Eyes on the deep

Scientists can send a remotely operated vehicle (ROV) into the dark. Pilots on a ship use its cameras and lights to observe wildlife, and its robotic arms to collect samples.

Sharing equipment, training, and observations helps more countries study deep-sea habitats and make informed decisions about their protection.

04 4,000–6,000 m

Abyssal Zone

Small flakes of food drift onto a vast, muddy plain.

Target 14.1 · Reduce pollution

Snow on the seafloor

Marine snow is a slow rain of organic material, including dead plankton and tiny waste particles. Sea pigs, a type of sea cucumber, walk across the flat, sediment-covered floor and feed on decaying material.

What else sinks this far?

Human-made litter reaches the deep seafloor too. Researchers found microplastics in sediment from a 4,800 m abyssal plain near the Philippines.

Ocean Status

In force 17 January 2026

Target 14.c · Cooperate through international law

High Seas Treaty

Also called the BBNJ Agreement, the treaty gives countries a shared process for setting up protected areas and assessing activities that could harm marine biodiversity beyond national borders.

05 6,000 m and deeper

Hadal Zone

The seabed drops into narrow, steep-sided trenches.

Target 14.a · Advance ocean science

Built for the pressure

The shrimp-like amphipod Hirondellea gigas lives near the bottom of the Mariana Trench. It has no gas-filled swim bladder to compress, and its cells function under pressure roughly 1,000 times that at sea level.

Deep-sea sampling has found synthetic particles in trench amphipods. Studying what reaches these animals helps scientists track how far pollution travels.

Sustainable Development Goal 14

Protect Life
Below Water

Rain can carry litter and other pollution from inland streets all the way to the coast. You can help keep it out of waterways, support healthier fisheries, and protect the places marine animals depend on.

Target 14.1

Use fewer disposables

Carry a refillable bottle or a reusable bag and skip single-use items you can do without. Bring your own food container when you can and check which plastics your local collection service accepts.

Target 14.1

Join a cleanup

Look for a scheduled river or coastal cleanup through a school, local council, or conservation group. Count what you collect by type so your group can see which items keep turning up.

Target 14.4

Choose responsible seafood

Ask what species you’re buying and where and how it was caught or farmed. Use those details to check a seafood guide or trusted certification. The same fish can come from fisheries with very different practices.

Target 14.1

Keep pollution out of drains

Take used oil, paint, and other household chemicals to a local collection point that accepts them. Use only the fertilizer your garden needs so rain carries fewer excess nutrients into waterways.

Target 14.2

Give reefs room to grow

When snorkeling or diving, keep your fins and hands clear of coral. Follow local rules on the water and use a designated mooring where available so anchors and chains stay off reefs and seagrass.

Target 14.3

Save energy and cut emissions

Switch off lights in empty rooms and walk, cycle, or share a ride when practical. Burning fewer fossil fuels cuts carbon dioxide emissions, which contribute to ocean warming and acidification.

What schools, communities, and governments can do

Schools and volunteer groups
Use cleanup records to decide what to change. If drink bottles keep turning up around campus, make a case for a refill station. Compare litter counts before and after it opens.
Local governments and residents
Improve waste collection and stormwater management, especially near streams and flood-prone areas. Work with local groups to protect and restore coastal habitats such as mangroves.
Fisheries and protected-area managers
Set catch limits using fish population surveys and give fishing communities a role in decisions. Fund monitoring and enforce rules in marine protected areas so managers can see whether habitats and wildlife are recovering.
